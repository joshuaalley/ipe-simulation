\textit{This document maps the textbook theory each phase teaches to what the
engine actually computes, and flags where the two diverge. It exists so you can
answer a sharp question and never overclaim. Students never see it.}\par
\noindent\rule{\textwidth}{0.4pt}

\section{Modeling philosophy}

The engine is a \textbf{pedagogical simulator}, not a computable general
equilibrium (CGE) or DSGE model. Every round is resolved by tractable
closed-form arithmetic --- Cobb-Douglas and CES aggregators, multiplicative
welfare adjustments, and reduced-form policy-to-state dynamics --- chosen so
students can \emph{act} and \emph{see} a directional result, deliberately
trading equilibrium rigor for legibility.

Two consequences worth holding in mind throughout:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Welfare is dimensionless utility}, not money. ``Money''
  quantities (debt, taxes, side payments) reach welfare only through a stated
  bridge --- valuing goods at \code{world\_prices} --- or are kept in a separate
  ledger that never touches utility.
  \item \textbf{Prices are not market-clearing.} Inter-country trade is barter
  at student-negotiated ratios; there is no endogenous price vector and no
  factor-price equalization. The engine demonstrates each model's qualitative
  lesson; it does not solve for equilibrium.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Notation}
$g$ indexes goods (cloth, wine, machinery); $j$ indexes the $J$ goods in welfare;
$L,K$ are labor and capital; $Y_g$ is output of good $g$; $c_j$ is consumption of
good $j$; $w,r$ are wage and return to capital; $D$ is a country's debt stock;
$d$ is its currency depreciation factor (1.00 = par).

\subsection{The welfare pipeline (order of operations each round)}
For each country the engine computes base utility, then applies multiplicative
adjustments in this fixed order (later phases only):
\[
U_{\text{base}} \xrightarrow{\text{Ph.5 crisis}} \xrightarrow{\text{Ph.7 hegemon cost}}
\xrightarrow{\text{Ph.7 global crisis}} \underbrace{U_{\text{trade}}}_{\text{gains-from-trade measured here}}
\xrightarrow{\text{Ph.6 debt}} U_{\text{final}}.
\]
Gains-from-trade are measured at $U_{\text{trade}}$ --- before debt --- so that
borrowing (consumption pulled from the future) is not miscounted as a gain from
trade.

\section{Phase 1 --- Ricardian comparative advantage}
\textbf{Theory.} One factor (labor), constant productivity per good. A country
exports the good in which it has lower opportunity cost. The PPF is a straight
line; gains from trade come from specialization.

\textbf{Engine} (\code{\_compute\_production}, \code{\_utility}).
\begin{align*}
Y_g &= L_g \cdot a_g && \text{(linear: } a_g \text{ = productivity of good } g\text{)}\\
U &= \prod_{j=1}^{J} c_j^{1/J} && \text{(Cobb-Douglas, equal weights)}
\end{align*}
Opportunity cost (shown in the briefs, 2-good case): $\text{OC} = a_{g_0}/a_{g_1}$.
Trade is barter; a tariff destroys a fraction of the imported good,
$\text{received} = \text{qty}\,(1-t)$.

\textbf{Divergence.} Linear production $\Rightarrow$ constant opportunity cost
(no diminishing returns, no curved PPF). ``Gains from trade'' is utility of the
consumed bundle minus utility of own production (the no-trade baseline), not a
welfare-theoretic equivalent variation.

\section{Phase 2 --- Heckscher-Ohlin}
\textbf{Theory.} Two factors. A country exports the good that intensively uses
its abundant factor. Stolper-Samuelson: opening trade raises the real return of
the abundant factor and lowers the scarce factor (the magnification effect:
$\hat{p}_1 \Rightarrow \hat{w} > \hat{p}_1 > 0 > \hat{r}$ when good 1 is
labor-intensive).

\textbf{Engine} (\code{\_compute\_production}, \code{\_shadow\_prices},
\code{\_compute\_factor\_prices}). Production is Cobb-Douglas; factor returns are
\emph{value} marginal products at implicit home prices taken from the
post-trade consumption bundle $c$:
\begin{align*}
Y_g &= \mathrm{TFP}_g \cdot L_g^{\alpha_g} K_g^{\beta_g}, \qquad \alpha_g + \beta_g = 1 \\
p_g &= \min\!\left(\frac{U}{c_g},\; 10\right), \qquad
  U = \Big(\prod_{j} c_j\Big)^{1/J}
  \qquad \text{(so } \textstyle\prod_j p_j^{1/J} = 1\text{)} \\
w_g &= p_g\,\alpha_g \frac{Y_g}{L_g}, \qquad
r_g = p_g\,\beta_g \frac{Y_g}{K_g}
\end{align*}
The reported $w$ and $r$ are allocation-weighted averages of $w_g$ and $r_g$.
$p_g$ is the Cobb-Douglas marginal utility $U/(Jc_g)$ rescaled so the ideal price
index is 1: a good the country consumes little of is dear at home, and returns
are measured in consumption bundles --- \emph{real} returns. In autarky $c = Y$,
so prices reflect domestic scarcity. Opening trade changes $c$, hence $p$, hence
$w$ and $r$: exporting the labor-intensive good raises its home price and with
it the wage. \emph{That is the Stolper-Samuelson channel.} The cap of 10 binds
only when a good is exported almost entirely.

\textbf{Why this form.} The engine originally reported \emph{physical} marginal
products, $w_g = \alpha_g Y_g / L_g$. Those depend only on the allocation, so
trade could never move them --- in class a country that traded with an unchanged
allocation showed identical factor prices to three decimals --- and averaging
cloth-units with machinery-units is not meaningful. A first fix scaled prices so
the bundle was worth its world-price value; nominal returns then swelled and
shrank with the gains from trade, and a capital-abundant country could show its
return to capital \emph{falling} when trade opened. Normalizing the price index
to 1 fixes that: in a controlled test with every allocation held fixed, the
labor-abundant countries' wages rise and capital returns fall, and the
capital-abundant country's capital return rises and wage falls, in absolute
real terms (\code{stress\_test.py}, \code{test\_stolper\_samuelson}). Histories
saved before this change keep the old numbers; replaying those rounds gives the
new ones.

\textbf{Divergence.} The prices are shadow prices read off a consumption bundle,
not market-clearing prices: there is no zero-profit condition, and because
students allocate factors freely, sector marginal products are not equalized ---
the reported $w$ and $r$ are averages across sectors, not a single economy-wide
factor price. No factor-price equalization. The magnification effect holds in
the controlled test but is not guaranteed for arbitrary play: when a country
reallocates its factors \emph{and} trades in the same round, the reallocation
effect and the trade effect are mixed together. To show Stolper-Samuelson
cleanly, compare a country whose allocation did not change. Trade is also barter
at whatever terms students negotiate, not balanced at common prices, so a
lopsided deal is a transfer: $w$ and $r$ value what the country
\emph{produces} at home prices, including goods shipped out for little in
return, and both can rise while welfare falls. (In class, a country that sent
27 cloth for 9 machinery saw its wage double while its welfare fell 23\%.)
Welfare, not $w$ and $r$, says whether a deal paid. The engine will not
reproduce, e.g., the Leontief paradox.

\section{Phase 3 --- MNCs, varieties, new trade theory}
\textbf{Theory.} Krugman: increasing returns $+$ love of variety yield
intra-industry trade and gains from variety, formalized with CES preferences and
monopolistic competition.

\textbf{Engine} (\code{\_compute\_firm\_production}, \code{\_build\_variety\_bundles},
\code{\_utility\_with\_varieties}, \code{\_compute\_firm\_profits}).
MNC output is linear in scale, $Y_{\text{firm}} = \text{scale}\times\text{productivity}$
(0 in a relocation round; scale capped at \code{max\_scale}). Each good's
consumption is a CES aggregate over its varieties, nested inside the Cobb-Douglas:
\[
c_g = \Big(\textstyle\sum_{v} q_v^{\rho}\Big)^{1/\rho}, \qquad
U = \prod_{g} c_g^{1/J}, \qquad \rho = 0.6,\ \ \sigma = \tfrac{1}{1-\rho} = 2.5 .
\]
More varieties raise $c_g$ at fixed total quantity --- the variety gain. Firm
profit (numeraire): $\pi = Y_{\text{firm}}\cdot p_{\text{industry}} -
\text{scale}\cdot\text{unit\_cost}$. Trade moves a good's varieties in proportion
to the exporter's variety mix.

\textbf{Divergence.} Real new trade theory has monopolistic competition with free
entry, markup pricing, and an \emph{endogenous} number of varieties pinned down by
market size and fixed costs. Here the firm set is fixed and CES enters only on the
consumption side; firms sell at a fixed world price, not a markup. The model
captures love-of-variety and intra-industry trade qualitatively, not the
monopolistic-competition equilibrium.

\section{Phase 4 --- Heterogeneous firms (Melitz)}
\textbf{Theory.} Firms draw productivity $\varphi$; only firms above a cutoff
$\varphi^\ast$ find it worth paying the fixed export cost $f_x$ and exporting.
Trade reallocates toward high-$\varphi$ firms and raises average productivity
(selection).

\textbf{Engine} (\code{\_compute\_firm\_profits}). Each firm has a fixed
productivity tier. With \code{export}\,=\,True it pays the fixed cost:
\[
\pi = \underbrace{Y_{\text{firm}}\,p_{\text{ind}}}_{\text{revenue}}
- \text{scale}\cdot\text{unit\_cost}
- \underbrace{f_x}_{\text{if exporting}}
- \underbrace{p_{\text{ind}}Y_{\text{firm}}\cdot \tau_{\text{mnc}}}_{\text{MNC tax, Ph.4+}} .
\]
Selection is demonstrated by outcome: a low-productivity firm that pays $f_x$
earns negative $\pi$; a high-productivity firm profits. Populist regimes set a
tariff floor (applied tariff $= \max(\text{declared},\ \text{floor})$) and an MNC
revenue tax routed to a \textbf{separate ledger} (it does not enter welfare).

\textbf{Divergence.} Real Melitz derives $\varphi^\ast$ endogenously from the
productivity distribution and fixed costs in equilibrium. Here productivity is
discrete (HIGH/MED/LOW) and the export choice is the student's, so selection is
shown, not solved.

\section{Political approval (all phases)}
\textbf{Theory.} In welfare terms a tariff is pure deadweight loss, so a
welfare-maximizing player would never protect --- which makes the politics of
Blocks 4, 6 and 7 unplayable. The missing half is the \emph{distributional}
one: protection is a transfer to the import-competing sector, whose gain is
concentrated and organized while the consumer's loss is diffuse (Olson). This
ledger supplies that political return without touching the utility function.

\textbf{Engine} (\code{\_update\_approval}, \code{\_resolve\_governments}).
For country $i$, with $M_i$ the value of imports actually received,
$\bar t_i$ the import-weighted mean tariff levied on them, $C_i$ consumption
capacity at world prices, $c_i$ the share of consumption paid to the groups
trade displaced, $S_i$ the \emph{net} goods another country sent
(received minus sent), and $A_i$ approval:
\begin{align*}
\text{exposure } x_i &= M_i / C_i, \qquad \bar t_i = \text{(tariff-weighted)}\\
\Delta A_i &= \underbrace{\pi\,\bar t_i x_i}_{\text{protection}}
 - \underbrace{\varepsilon\, x_i}_{\text{openness}}
 + \underbrace{\text{clip}\!\left(\rho \tfrac{U_i - U_i'}{U_i'}\right)}_{\text{prosperity}}
 + \underbrace{\kappa\,(c_i + S_i / C_i)}_{\text{compensation}}
 + \underbrace{\delta\,(A_0 - A_i)}_{\text{drift}}\\
A_i &\leftarrow \operatorname{clip}\left(A_i + \Delta A_i,\; 0,\; 100\right)
\end{align*}
with $\pi=35$, $\varepsilon=14$, $\rho=35$ (clipped to $\pm 15$), $\kappa=25$,
$\delta=0.10$, $A_0=50$, and $U_i'$ last round's welfare.

\textbf{Buying off the losers.} $c_i \le 0.25$ is a decision, taken on the same
form as tariffs and available in every phase. It costs welfare ---
$U_i \mathrel{\times}= (1 - 0.20\,c_i)$, the deadweight on taxing and transferring
--- but leaves trade open, so at a typical import exposure it buys about the
approval a 20\% tariff does for roughly two-thirds of the welfare, and without
shrinking anyone's trade. That is the ``compensate rather than protect'' move of
Block 7 made playable. Side payments ($S_i$) are goods one country hands another,
also available from Phase 1 on; only the \emph{net} counts, so two countries
cannot pass the same crate back and forth and both bank approval for it.

\textbf{The endogenous backlash.} If $A_i < 30$ for two consecutive rounds the
government falls: \code{inject\_populist\_backlash} fires automatically (20\%
tariff floor, 10\% MNC tax) and $A_i$ resets to $A_0$. Protection is therefore
imposed \emph{on} a country that ignores its losers --- the instructor does not
choose the victim.

\textbf{Tuning.} Holding policy fixed, approval converges to
\[
A^{*} = A_0 - \frac{x\,(\varepsilon - \pi\bar t)}{\delta},
\]
so the steady state, not the starting value, decides who survives. The
coefficients are set so $A^{*}$ sits below the crisis floor under free trade
($\approx 20$) and above it from roughly a 20\% tariff ($\approx 35$). Under
uniform play over a 20-round term this yields: free trade highest welfare but
one government falls; 20\% tariffs all survive at $\sim3\%$ less welfare; 40\%
tariffs politically safe at $\sim12\%$ less. \emph{An earlier tuning put
$A^{*}$ below the floor at every tariff level, so every trading country fell
regardless of policy --- a doom clock rather than a decision. Check $A^{*}$,
not the first few rounds, after changing any coefficient.}

\textbf{Divergence.} This is the most openly \emph{reduced-form} component in
the engine --- a stipulated response function, in the same family as trilemma
stress and the debt risk premium, not something derived from preferences or
voting. There is no median voter, no electoral rule, and no separate interest
groups: the ``concentrated benefit'' is a coefficient, not an aggregation over
agents. It also has no free-rider problem and no lobbying cost, so it dramatizes
Olson's logic rather than modelling it. Approval never enters $U$, so all welfare
and gains-from-trade figures remain exactly as documented above; it is a second
scoreboard, deliberately in tension with the first.

\section{Phase 5 --- Money, FX, and the trilemma}
\textbf{Theory.} The Mundell-Fleming ``impossible trinity'': a country cannot
simultaneously run a fixed exchange rate, open capital markets, and independent
monetary policy. Pushing all three invites a speculative attack.

\textbf{Engine} (\code{\_apply\_monetary\_decisions}, \code{\_compute\_fx\_friction},
\code{\_weak\_fx\_cost}). Each round a country picks a regime (peg or float), capital
controls (on or off) and money growth $g\in\{0,2,5,10\%\}$. Printing ($g>0$)
\emph{is} independent monetary policy; $g=0$ follows the anchor. Let $s$ be the
stress counter and $d$ the FX index (depreciation factor, $1=$ par). Overreach is
peg $\wedge$ open capital $\wedge$ $g>0$. Per round:
\[
\text{overreach} \Rightarrow s \mathrel{+}= 1; \qquad \text{else } s = 0 .
\]
\[
s = 1:\ d \mathrel{\times}= 0.90\ \text{(warning)};\qquad
s = 2:\ d \mathrel{\times}= 0.70,\ U \mathrel{\times}= 0.90,\ s \to 0\ \text{(crisis)} .
\]
What each choice buys or costs:
\begin{align*}
\text{printing } g>0:&\quad d \mathrel{\times}= (1-g), \qquad
  U \mathrel{\times}= (1 + \sigma\, g\, d), \quad \sigma = 1 \\
\text{weak currency:}&\quad \text{each import received} \times
  \big(1 - \lambda\,(1-d_{\text{receiver}})\big), \quad \lambda = 0.5 \\
  &\quad U \mathrel{\times}= \big(1 - \mu\,(1-d)\big) \text{ every round}, \quad \mu = 0.10 \\
\text{capital controls:}&\quad \text{hosted firms' output} \times (1-0.15),
  \ \text{no foreign borrowing} \\
\text{FX friction:}&\quad \mathrm{fx} = 0.02 \ (+0.03 \text{ just after a warning})
\end{align*}
FX friction is zero with the reserve holder, inside a monetary union, or between
two pegged currencies. Friction and the weak-currency cut stack on tariffs
multiplicatively:
$\text{received} = \text{qty}\,(1-t)(1-\mathrm{fx})\big(1-\lambda(1-d)\big)$. The
stimulus and the drag are applied after the gains-from-trade measure (neither is a
gain from trade); the drag hits any currency below par, printer or crisis victim.
Crisis targeting (\code{fx\_vulnerability}) scores overreach, stress, a
recent warning, an open peg, loose money, open capital and a weak currency; a dead
heat at the top, or no exposure at all, fires nothing. \code{"managed"} is accepted
as a synonym for \code{"float"}.

\textbf{Why this form.} Under the original rules printing only depreciated the
currency, independence only enabled printing, capital controls cost nothing, and
\code{managed} behaved exactly like \code{float} --- so peg $+$ controls $+$ 0\%
was never worse than any other choice, and the trilemma never bit. Each option now
buys something. The stimulus is scaled by $d$ because a flat stimulus made printing
10\% every round the best strategy even as the currency sank to a quarter of par;
the import cost falls on the printer itself, because a cost borne only by foreign
firm owners would not deter it; and the welfare drag exists because, in a world
with little or no trade, the import cost barely bites and printing forever won
again. \code{stress\_test\_money\_balance.py} plays six quite different worlds
(class-like, heavy, light and no trade, specialized, six countries) and fails if
printing forever or peg $+$ controls $+$ 0\% is ever a country's best choice, if
one choice is best for everybody in a world, or if controls fall more than 12\%
behind. The constants sit in the middle of the passing range (it holds with
$\sigma$ and $\mu$ both scaled by $\pm 25\%$ and with $\lambda$ from 0.35 to 0.65),
so a real class's trade pattern should not need a re-tune. In that test, printing
every round pays only for economies that barely trade; in trading worlds the best
fixed stance is a peg or a float at 0\%, depending on partners --- the
small-open-economy lesson --- and printing is a one-off tool.

\textbf{Divergence.} $d$ is a \emph{reduced-form currency-strength index} driven by
policy choices and shocks --- there is no money supply/demand, no interest parity,
no PPP, no modeled exchange-rate market. The stimulus is a reduced-form short-run
gain from monetary expansion (no Phillips curve, no output gap); scaling it by $d$
stands in for lost credibility. The trilemma is operationalized as a stress
accumulator, not derived from an open-economy equilibrium. A monetary union shares
one $(s,d,\text{policy})$ state across members (individual debts, shared currency
--- the Eurozone analogy).

\section{Phase 6 --- Sovereign debt}
\textbf{Theory.} Debt is sustainable while the interest burden stays within
capacity; spreads rise with the debt burden; ``original sin'' (Eichengreen-Hausmann)
means foreign-currency debt becomes crushing when the local currency depreciates.

\textbf{Engine} (\code{\_apply\_country\_debt}). Let consumption capacity
$C = \sum_g c_g\, p_g$ (goods at world prices). Then:
\begin{align*}
\text{rate} &= \underbrace{0.05}_{\text{base}} + \underbrace{0.15}_{\text{premium}}\cdot \frac{D}{C}, \qquad
\text{interest} = D\cdot\text{rate} \\
\text{borrow } B:&\quad U \mathrel{\times}= \Big(1 + \tfrac{B}{C}\Big), \quad
  B \le 0.25\,C, \quad B = 0 \text{ under capital controls} \\
\text{service } (\text{interest}+\text{repay}):&\quad
U \mathrel{\times}= \max\!\Big(0,\ 1 - \tfrac{\text{service}/d}{C}\Big), \qquad
D \leftarrow D + B - \text{repay} \\
\text{default}:&\quad U \mathrel{\times}= 1 - \min\!\Big(1, 1.25\,\tfrac{D}{C}\Big),
  \qquad D \to 0
\end{align*}
The division of service by $d$ \emph{is} original sin: a weak currency ($d<1$)
inflates the real burden of hard-currency debt. \textbf{Default} costs 1.25 times
the debt's face value (\emph{not} divided by $d$), bans borrowing for 2 rounds and
adds trade friction. At par it therefore costs more than repaying; after a large
devaluation ($1/d$ well above 1.25, a fall of roughly 20\% or more) it escapes the
magnification --- which is when walking away pays. In \code{final\_round} there is no new borrowing and the
whole stock is repaid (or defaulted on). \textbf{IMF bailout}:
$D \mathrel{\times}= 0.5$ now, in exchange for $U \mathrel{\times}= 0.92$ for 2
rounds (austerity).

\textbf{Why this form.} Borrowing used to be capped at $C$ (enough to double a
round's welfare) and default cost only the ban and the friction: in a six-round
test, a country that borrowed the maximum and defaulted whenever it could beat
never borrowing by a third, and borrowing in the final round was free. With the
25\% cap, the 1.25$\times$ default cost and the final-round settlement, in all five
trading test worlds serial default loses 1.5--3\% to never borrowing, repaying beats
defaulting at par by about 1\% of the six-round total, and after a 40\%
devaluation default beats repaying in full --- the Round 22 lesson
(\code{stress\_test\_money\_balance.py}).

\textbf{Divergence.} No inter-temporal optimization (no Euler equation, no
forward-looking default decision); the borrow/service welfare scaling is a
mechanical multiplier. Debt is denominated only in the reserve currency. The rate
rule is a reduced-form spread, not a default-probability-priced yield.

\section{Phase 7 --- Institutions and power}
\textbf{Theory.} Hegemonic stability (Kindleberger, Krasner): a leader supplies
public goods --- open markets, a stable system --- at private cost, and is tempted
to free-ride. Regimes/the WTO (Keohane) lower transaction costs among members.

\textbf{Engine} (\code{\_institutional\_friction\_delta}, \code{\_global\_crisis\_factor},
\code{challenge\_hegemon}). All institutional effects act on \emph{trade friction}
or welfare. The friction added to a trade leg, on top of the Phase-5 base and any
Phase-6 default friction, is
\[
\Delta_{\text{inst}} =
\underbrace{\big({-}0.05 \text{ if provide else } {+}0.07\big)}_{\text{hegemon public good}}
\;\;\underbrace{-\,0.02}_{\substack{\text{WTO dividend, if both members}\\\text{\& neither defected}\\(\times 0.5 \text{ if debt-banned})}} ,
\]
then the total $\mathrm{fx}$ is clamped to $[0,1]$. The hegemon pays
$U \mathrel{\times}= 0.96$ when it provides. A pending \textbf{global crisis} of
severity $\varsigma$ scales every country's welfare by
\[
1 - \varsigma\cdot\big(0.5 \text{ if hegemon provides}\big)\cdot\big(1 - 0.5\,\text{wto\_share}\big),
\]
so leadership and broad membership both soften it. A \textbf{hegemonic challenge}
succeeds when the challenger coalition's cumulative welfare exceeds that of all
non-coalition countries; hegemony and the reserve-currency role then transfer.
Side payments are goods transfers applied before welfare.

\textbf{Divergence.} A stylized public-goods/coordination model, not a
game-theoretic equilibrium. The challenge rule is a raw weight comparison, not a
bargaining solution; ``defection'' is simply applying a tariff above a binding.
The hegemony parameters are tuned (see the simulation notes) so that provision is
collectively beneficial in both WTO-none and WTO-all settings while the hegemon is
still individually tempted to free-ride.
